How to Track Your Perfume Collection (Wear Logs & More)

Tracking your perfume collection reveals what you actually wear, exposes neglected bottles, and builds your taste profile. Here's exactly how to do it.

By Perfume Picks · Published July 13, 2026

Quick Answer

Track your perfume collection by logging every bottle (name, house, concentration, size, date acquired) and recording each wear with notes on occasion, performance, and mood. A spreadsheet, dedicated app, or paper journal all work, the key is consistency. Wear data reveals what you actually reach for and exposes bottles quietly collecting dust.

If you own more than a handful of bottles, you already know the feeling: you spray something, love it, and then promptly forget about it for six months. A lot of value sits unused in the average collection, bottles that got bought, loved briefly, then quietly forgotten on the shelf. Tracking your collection is the direct fix. It turns a passive shelf display into an active wardrobe you actually use.

Why does tracking matter, isn’t just owning the bottles enough?

Perfume as a hobby is largely unstructured. Unlike wine or food, there’s no universally accepted methodology for tracking, reviewing, or analyzing your collection, which means you’re building the system yourself.

That gap is the opportunity. A simple tracking habit gives you data that no amount of browsing Fragrantica ever will: your wear patterns, your neglected bottles, your repeating preferences.

The payoff is concrete. Once you know a particular woody oriental gets picked up two or three times a week while a beloved citrus sits untouched for months, you can make smarter decisions: rotating deliberately, building a more intentional fragrance wardrobe, and eventually buying fewer bottles you’ll regret. The shift toward fragrance wardrobes of 6-10 bottles is already happening, but plenty of buyers still regret at least one fragrance purchase. Wear tracking is one of the clearest ways to reduce that number over time.

What to log for each bottle in your collection

Collectors get paralyzed trying to design the perfect system before they’ve tracked a single bottle. Keep it simple to start.

For each bottle, capture:

  • Fragrance name and house
  • Concentration (EDT, EDP, Parfum)
  • Bottle size (ml) and fill level
  • Date acquired and approximate price paid
  • Fragrance family (floral, woody, oriental, fresh, etc.)
  • Your rating (a 1-10 or 1-5 scale works fine)
  • Status: full bottle / decant / sample

Beyond the basics, useful optional fields include category (designer, niche, indie, celebrity), year of release, your written impressions, and the listed notes. One rule: choose only the fields you actually find valuable. This system is for you, and it should only contain information useful to you.

What should a single wear log entry look like?

People track their collection but forget to track how they use it, which is where the real intelligence lives.

For each wear, record:

  • Date
  • Fragrance worn
  • Occasion (work, casual, evening, date, travel)
  • Weather or season
  • Number of sprays and where applied
  • Quick performance notes: longevity, projection, how it felt that day

For daily wears, try noting your impressions at first spray, an hour in, and at the drydown, then log it alongside base information about the perfume itself. That three-stage snapshot takes under two minutes and produces far richer data than a simple star rating. After a few weeks, patterns emerge fast.

Using wear data to figure out which bottles to keep

This is where tracking pays off most visibly. Pull up your wear log after 60-90 days and run a simple tally: how many times did each bottle get worn? The result is almost always lopsided.

Collectors consistently find that a small portion of their collection accounts for most of their wears, a real-world parallel to the 80/20 principle. The bottles clustered at the bottom of your wear count fall into one of three categories:

  1. Genuinely unloved: you rated them high once, but skin chemistry or season never clicked. Candidates for selling or swapping.
  2. Neglected gems: you love them but keep forgetting they exist. This is a rotation problem, not a bottle problem. Deliberately scheduling them for a week resolves it.
  3. Seasonal sleepers: heavy orientals in July, crisp aquatics in December. Log the season alongside each wear and you’ll see these naturally cluster. They’re not neglected, just waiting.

Tracking wears in even a basic bullet journal, one mark per wear, lets you see what you’re reaching for and how often, so you know when to rotate or clear out a bottle before it ages out.

How do I track performance, not just that I wore it, but how it performed?

Longevity and projection ratings are only useful when they’re contextual.

One of the biggest factors influencing performance is the surface you apply to. Your skin chemistry, pH, temperature, oiliness, and hydration, interacts with fragrance molecules directly, causing them to develop, project, and fade differently. Dry skin, for instance, tends to absorb fragrance faster. That means a “poor longevity” note logged on a cold winter morning after a long flight tells a different story than the same fragrance logged on a humid summer afternoon on well-moisturized skin.

Build two performance fields into your log: a longevity score (hours until it’s gone) and a projection score (intimate / moderate / beast mode). Then add one free-text line for context: skin condition, weather, how many sprays. After 10-15 wears of the same bottle across different conditions, you’ll have a reliable performance picture no review site can give you, because it’s yours, on your skin.

The fastest way to get an existing collection logged without starting from scratch

The backlog feels overwhelming when you’re looking at 20 or 30 bottles.

Batch the inventory in one sitting. 30-45 minutes is usually enough for even a large collection. Work through the physical bottles one by one and enter only the non-negotiable fields: name, house, concentration, size. That’s it. Resist the urge to add ratings and notes for bottles you haven’t worn recently; those get filled in organically as you wear and log going forward.

Sort by house from the beginning, it saves a painful reorganization later. Once the inventory exists, wear logging takes seconds per entry. The first 30-day wear report, when it arrives, is genuinely eye-opening for most collectors.

5+ FAQs

What information should I log for each perfume bottle? At minimum: fragrance name, house, concentration (EDT/EDP/Parfum), bottle size, date acquired, and a personal rating. Useful extras include price paid, fragrance family, notes listed, and whether it’s a full bottle, decant, or sample.

How often should I update my perfume wear log? Log each wear the same day you apply it, even a quick note is enough. Trying to reconstruct a week’s worth of wears from memory is unreliable, and the whole point of a log is accurate data over time.

Is a spreadsheet or a dedicated app better for tracking perfumes? Spreadsheets offer complete flexibility and no cost, but require manual setup and discipline. Dedicated apps handle the database work for you and make wear logging faster on mobile. If you own more than 15-20 bottles, an app typically saves time in the long run.

What’s the most useful insight a wear log gives you? Your most-worn versus least-worn ratio. Collectors consistently find a small cluster of bottles accounts for the majority of their wears. That gap tells you what to rotate, sell, or prioritize.

Do I need to track samples and decants, or just full bottles? Track whatever you wear. Samples and decants count as real wears and influence your taste profile just as much as full bottles. A simple status column (sample / decant / full bottle) keeps them organized without extra effort.

Can tracking my collection help me figure out my taste profile? Yes, and it’s one of the most reliable ways to do it. When wear data accumulates across enough bottles, the fragrance families and notes you consistently reach for become statistically obvious. That pattern is your taste profile, derived from actual behavior rather than self-reported preference. Perfume Picks builds this automatically in the background as you log wears, surfacing it as a personal scent profile.
