Perfume makes a generous gift. It's personal, it's sensory, and it signals that you thought about the person. And yet there's a persistent belief, stretching across multiple cultures and continents, that wrapping up a bottle of fragrance is asking for trouble.

## "I heard it's bad luck to give someone perfume, is that actually true?"

*A question we hear often:* it depends entirely on where you're from.

In Indian superstition, giving perfume is considered bad luck. In Greek tradition, the recipient can ward off any ill effect by returning a small coin to the giver. In Chinese folk belief, gifting perfume is said to attract a third party and cause a breakup. These are genuine, living cultural traditions, and they matter because a lot of fragrance collectors have family members or partners who take them seriously.

They are regional folk beliefs, not global rules. In Brazil, France, the UK, and most of the United States, giving someone a beautiful bottle of perfume is a kind act. The superstition is culturally significant in some communities, and knowing about it makes you a more considerate gift-giver. It carries no universal weight.

## "Where does the superstition actually come from? What's the logic?"

In several cultures, including parts of India, gifting perfume is thought to cause a relationship to fade, just as a scent fades over time. This belief is especially common among older, more traditional family members.

The logic links the short life of a scent to the idea of temporary affection. A fragrance disappears within hours, so the gift is said to carry that quality into the relationship. Giving someone perfume can also read as a comment on how they smell, which makes it feel rude rather than unlucky in certain contexts.

The symbolism runs the other way elsewhere. Perfumes are associated with good fortune in many traditions, particularly when gifted at weddings and festivals, where the gesture is considered to bring prosperity to the recipient. So even within Indian cultural contexts, the belief is not uniform.

## "Is there a way to 'fix' the bad luck if I still want to give perfume?"

*This one comes up a lot,* especially from collectors who've found a perfect bottle for someone but are worried about causing offence.

The fix is low-tech. The recipient hands back a small coin, usually one rupee, to the giver. That exchange symbolically turns the gift into a purchase. Once it happens, the superstition no longer applies, and the perfume is treated as a bought item rather than a gift.

In Greek tradition, the same principle holds: the recipient gives back any single coin. The value doesn't matter.

Knowing this ritual lets you give the gift you wanted to give while respecting what matters to the recipient's family. A single coin tucked inside the gift box, with a note explaining the tradition, turns a potentially awkward moment into a good one.

## "Which cultures observe this belief and which don't?"

Here's a quick reference for collectors who regularly give fragrance as gifts:

| Region / Culture | Belief | Common Workaround |
|---|---|---|
| Parts of India | Gifting perfume brings bad luck, especially to romantic partners | Recipient gives back a small coin (e.g. 1 rupee) |
| Greece | Perfume gift can ruin a relationship between giver and recipient | Recipient returns any single coin to the giver |
| Parts of China | Gifting perfume attracts a third party and causes breakups | Avoid gifting to romantic partners, or frame as a shared purchase |
| Brazil, France, UK, most of US | No widespread superstition; perfume is a standard luxury gift | No workaround needed |
| Weddings & festivals (many South Asian traditions) | Perfume is considered auspicious and brings good fortune | Gift freely, no coin required |

The pattern is worth noticing: where the superstition exists, it's almost always tied to romantic relationships, not friendships or professional ones. Giving a fragrance to a friend, colleague, or family member is rarely flagged as problematic even in cultures that observe the romantic superstition.

## "As a collector, should I even give perfume as a gift, it seems like a minefield?"

*Readers frequently ask* whether fragrance is worth the risk as a gift category at all.

It is, with one caveat that has nothing to do with superstition. The bigger practical challenge isn't bad luck; it's the personal nature of scent. What smells extraordinary on your skin may land flat or unpleasant on someone else's, because skin chemistry alters how a fragrance develops. Our guide on [how body chemistry affects perfume](/how-body-chemistry-affects-perfume) covers the science.

Experienced fragrance collectors tend to give in one of three ways. They give something the recipient has explicitly said they want, a specific bottle they've mentioned or a fragrance they've run out of. Or they give a sample or discovery set, letting the recipient choose what to commit to rather than imposing a full bottle. Or they give a gift card to a trusted retailer or niche perfume house, which respects the recipient's autonomy entirely. If you're buying a full bottle blind, our guide on [how to choose perfume as a gift](/how-to-choose-perfume-as-a-gift) has a framework for making that call.

The global fragrance market was valued at over $50 billion in 2023, and gift purchases account for a significant share of that volume, meaning tens of millions of perfume gifts change hands every year. The superstition is culturally interesting. It shouldn't stop you from giving one of the most considered, sensory gifts available.

## "What if someone gives me perfume and I come from a culture where it's considered bad luck?"

This is the more delicate situation, because you're the recipient, not the giver, and you may not want to explain a folk belief to someone who's trying to do something kind.

The coin exchange solves this gracefully. Thank the person warmly, accept the gift, and hand them any small coin from your wallet. Frame it as a fun tradition if they ask, or just do it without comment. Research on scent and memory consistently supports the idea that a beloved fragrance becomes a strong anchor to the person who gave it. The psychology works against the superstition.

If the perfume itself isn't to your taste, that's a separate challenge. Our guide on [what to do with perfume you don't like](/what-to-do-with-perfume-you-dont-like) has seven practical options that don't involve pretending to love something you'll never wear.

## "Does it matter which perfume you give, do some carry different symbolic weight?"

*A question we hear often* from collectors who want to be thoughtful about both the fragrance and its meaning.

There's no universal rule. In some traditions, gifting perfume before a journey is considered inauspicious, so people avoid it in those situations. Beyond that specific timing concern, the choice of fragrance carries more emotional weight than symbolic weight in most contexts.

Heavy, long-lasting fragrances, oud, amber, patchouli-forward compositions, tend to land better as gifts because their staying power is immediately noticeable. Lighter citrus or fresh scents can seem underwhelming on first spray, even when they're well-constructed. If you're giving to someone who isn't a collector, something with immediate warmth tends to work better than a challenging niche interpretation. And if the recipient collects seriously, the safest gift is a conversation first: ask what they've been curious about, or what they've run out of. ## Frequently asked questions

**Is it bad luck to give perfume as a gift?**
Only in certain cultural traditions. In parts of India and China, gifting perfume is linked to fading relationships. In Greek tradition, the bad luck can be neutralised by the recipient returning a small coin to the giver. Most cultures treat perfume as a perfectly normal and thoughtful gift.

**Why is giving perfume as a gift considered bad luck?**
The core idea is symbolic: scent fades, so a gifted perfume is said to represent a relationship or affection that will also fade. Some versions add that the perfume will attract a third party, causing a breakup. These are folk metaphors, not factual predictions.

**How do you break the bad luck of gifting perfume?**
In Greek and Indian traditions, the simplest fix is for the recipient to hand back a small coin, even a single low-value coin, to the giver. This symbolically converts the gift into a "purchase," removing the superstition's power. Some families use one rupee; others use any spare coin.

**Can you gift perfume to a romantic partner?**
Yes, the vast majority of people do so without any concern. If your partner or their family comes from a culture where the superstition is observed, simply include a small coin with the gift and ask them to "pay" you for it. It's a sweet ritual that acknowledges the tradition without avoiding the gift.

**What is the best way to give perfume as a gift for a collector?**
For a serious collector, a sample set, a decant of a hard-to-find fragrance, or a full bottle of something they've specifically mentioned wanting are the most appreciated options. Avoid blind-buying a full bottle of a scent they haven't tried, because even the most gifted nose can be surprised by a fragrance on their own skin.
