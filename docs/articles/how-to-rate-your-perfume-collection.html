How to Rate Your Perfume Collection (A Collector's Guide)

Learn how to build a personal fragrance rating system that actually helps you wear more, buy smarter, and understand your own taste. A practical guide for collectors.

By Perfume Picks · Published August 15, 2026

Quick Answer

Rate each fragrance across four dimensions, scent appeal, longevity, projection, and versatility, using a consistent personal scale (1, 5 or 1, 10). Log ratings soon after wearing, revisit scores seasonally, and let the data guide what you reach for, what you rotate out, and what gaps remain in your collection.

A personal rating system is one of the lowest-effort, highest-payoff habits a fragrance collector can build. Once you have scores attached to every bottle, you stop second-guessing what to reach for, start spotting patterns in your own taste, and make smarter buying decisions going forward. The problem is that most collectors either skip rating entirely or use a gut-feel approach that doesn’t hold up once the collection gets large.

Why would I bother rating fragrances I already own?

A question we hear often: especially from collectors who trust their memory. The short answer is that memory is optimistic and inconsistent, and collections grow faster than recall.

As a collection grows, written notes and scores become essential reminders, letting you compare fragrances against one another in a way that feels held together by something more than mood.

Without a rating system, you’re running your wardrobe on vibes. You’ll repeatedly reach for the same four bottles while a dozen others sit untouched, not because they’re bad, but because you haven’t given your brain an organized reason to consider them.

Ratings also make your perfume collection audit dramatically easier. Instead of holding up each bottle and trying to feel whether it sparks joy, you have actual data. Low-rated bottles become sell or swap candidates. Highly-rated bottles in the same fragrance family reveal your actual taste, which is often different from what you think your taste is. Industry data shows that 67% of buyers regret at least one fragrance purchase, and a consistent rating habit is one of the most effective ways to break that cycle.

What dimensions should I actually score?

This one comes up a lot. Giving a fragrance a single number feels intuitive, but a single score collapses too much information into one figure. A fragrance can smell extraordinary and last only two hours. That’s different from one that smells average but projects magnificently and lasts all day. Both might score a 7/10 overall, yet they serve completely different roles in your wardrobe.

The most useful framework scores four dimensions separately:

DimensionWhat to EvaluateWhy It Matters
Scent AppealDoes the composition genuinely move you?The core reason you own any bottle
LongevityHow many hours does it remain detectable on your skin?Affects where and when you reach for it
Projection / SillageHow far does it radiate? How does the trail behave?Determines office vs. evening suitability
VersatilityHow many seasons, occasions, or moods does it suit?Guides how often it earns a spot in rotation

One popular approach among serious collectors assigns 0, 20 points to overall scent impression, 0, 10 to projection and sillage, 0, 10 to longevity, and 0, 10 to value, yielding a total out of 50. That level of granularity works well for large collections. For most collectors, a simpler 1, 10 per dimension, summed or averaged, is enough precision without becoming a chore.

A 100-point framework divides into fragrance quality (0, 30), longevity (0, 30), sillage (0, 20), and packaging (0, 20), which is a useful structure if you want scores that reflect the full ownership experience rather than just the scent alone.

Which scale is actually best, stars, numbers, or something else?

Readers frequently ask whether to use a 5-star, 10-point, or letter-grade scale. There’s no universal right answer, but there are real trade-offs.

A 10-point scale is common in Western culture for art and academics, but a 5-star system works particularly well for perfumery. It looks cleaner, is widely understood, and its midpoint (3 stars) reads as a positive. On a 10-point scale, a 6 or 6.5 is psychologically perceived as negative, even if you intend it as neutral.

That difference matters when you’re building a personal log. On a 5-star scale, “3 = good but not essential” is easy to internalize. On a 10-point scale, collectors often cluster scores between 7 and 9, which feels meaningful but actually compresses everything into a useless range. If you find yourself rarely going below 7, defining what each score means before you start is what keeps objective rating possible.

A practical fix: write out your definitions before you score anything. For example:

  • 1, 2: Scrubber. Actively unpleasant; would sell or swap immediately.
  • 3, 4: Mediocre. Wearable but unlikely to reach for voluntarily.
  • 5, 6: Solid. Enjoyable in the right context, no urgency to replace.
  • 7, 8: Love. Regular rotation, would rebuy.
  • 9, 10: Holy grail. Would panic-buy backups.

The definitions matter far less than applying them the same way every time.

When is the right time to rate a fragrance?

This one comes up a lot. Timing your ratings properly is what separates useful data from noise.

A first-wear score is valuable as a baseline. Log it immediately after the day ends, while impressions are fresh. But don’t treat it as final. Many fragrances take several wears to reveal themselves, especially once the novelty wears off. Longevity, character, and projection can all shift depending on season and skin condition. What feels like a 5 at first may genuinely perform as an 8 later.

Best practice: give a provisional score after the first wear, flag it for review, and come back after 3, 5 wears in different conditions. A heavy amber that felt oppressive in July might become your most-reached-for bottle in November. A quick pass through your collection every six months keeps your scores honest and your rotation reflective of what you actually think now, not what you thought at purchase.

How do I handle fragrances that smell great but don’t last?

This is where dimension scoring earns its keep. A fragrance with a 9/10 scent appeal but a 4/10 longevity isn’t a bad fragrance. It’s a specific tool. You’d reach for it on a calm, indoor day when you’re wearing it for yourself, not a twelve-hour outdoor event.

Longevity ratings should be based on how long the fragrance lasts from application to the point it fades below a detectable threshold. You can factor in price context too: an inexpensive fragrance lasting only four hours warrants a low-average longevity score, while an expensive one performing the same way earns a low score outright.

A fragrance with mismatched dimension scores, say high scent appeal and low longevity, might be a candidate for layering over a scentless base, or for occasions where you only need 3, 4 hours of wear. Your rating captures that nuance. A single overall score doesn’t.

How do ratings help me figure out what to buy next?

This is where a consistent rating habit pays its biggest dividend. Sort your top-rated bottles (anything 7 and above) by fragrance family or dominant note, and patterns show up fast. Maybe every fragrance in your 8, 10 range is woody-amber. Maybe your florals cluster in the 5, 6 range. That data tells you something your gut probably won’t: you might not actually love florals as much as you buy them.

Most collectors today build wardrobes of 6, 10 bottles to suit different moods, routines, and occasions rather than sticking to one signature. Ratings let you assess whether each of those slots is filled by something you genuinely love or just something you happen to own. Gaps become obvious. If your eveningwear slot has nothing rated above a 6, that’s a targeted, justified reason to shop.

Perfume Picks uses your wear history to surface collection-aware picks from bottles you already own, meaning the more you log, the better the recommendations get. A wear log combined with personal ratings gives the app, and you, a real picture of which bottles earn their shelf space.

Do my ratings need to be private, or is it worth sharing them?

Readers frequently ask whether personal ratings have any value beyond their own collection management. They serve you best as a private, honest record. The moment you start thinking about how a rating looks to others, you lose the analytical honesty that makes the habit useful.

Community platforms like Fragrantica, Parfumo, and Basenotes aggregate crowd ratings, which are useful for spotting consensus and for blind buy research. But crowd scores reflect the average of many different skin chemistries, nose types, and taste profiles. Rating fragrances is inherently subjective. Your private scores, consistently applied, are a better signal for your wardrobe decisions than any community average.

That said, writing a brief private note alongside each rating, two or three sentences on the occasion, the season, how it performed, turns a number into a memory. Reviews and notes serve a dual purpose: they’re informative for anyone who cares about your observations, and they’re the only reliable way to remember your impressions over time. Even if no one else ever reads them, you will, and six months from now, you’ll be glad you did.

FAQs

What’s the best scale to use when rating perfumes? A 1, 10 scale gives you enough granularity to separate similar fragrances, while a 1, 5 star system is simpler and faster. The most important thing is consistency. Pick one scale and stick with it so scores stay comparable over time.

Should I rate a perfume the first time I wear it? Not definitively. A first-wear rating is useful as a baseline, but wait at least 2, 3 full wears across different conditions, seasons, occasions, moods, before locking in a final score. Many fragrances reveal depth (or flaws) over time.

Do I need to rate every dimension separately or just give an overall score? Separate dimension scores (scent, longevity, projection, versatility) are more actionable because they tell you why you feel the way you do about a fragrance. An overall score alone doesn’t help you decide whether to sell a bottle or simply reach for it on different occasions.

How often should I revisit and update my ratings? Seasonally is a good cadence. A fragrance you rated a 6/10 in summer may genuinely perform as an 8/10 in winter. Taste also shifts. Revisiting ratings every six months keeps your collection data accurate and useful.

Can ratings help me decide what to buy next? Yes. When you sort your top-rated bottles by note family or fragrance family, patterns emerge that reveal your true taste preferences, not just what you think you like. That makes future purchases far more targeted and reduces blind-buy regret.
