Most conversations about perfume value start and end at the sticker price. That's the wrong place to look. A $40 bottle you spray four times and forget costs you $10 a wear. A $220 niche EDP you reach for every weekday morning for a year might cost you under a dollar a wear. Cost per wear is the metric that actually tells you what a fragrance is worth, and once you understand it, buying decisions get a lot clearer.

## Wait, what even is "cost per wear" for perfume - is it the same as for clothes?

The principle is identical to the fashion concept: divide what you paid by how many times you use it. For clothing that's straightforward. For perfume, you need one extra step because you're not wearing the whole bottle at once. You're burning through it spray by spray.

The core formula is:

Cost per wear = Bottle price / Realistic number of wears

And realistic number of wears comes from:

Wears = Total sprays in bottle / Your sprays per use

A reliable working estimate is 10 sprays per ml of perfume. This varies slightly by atomizer design, but it holds well across most modern bottles. So a 50 ml bottle contains approximately 500 sprays, and a 100 ml bottle approximately 1,000. Divide those totals by how many sprays you typically use per occasion and you have your denominator.

Industry estimates put one spray at roughly 0.1 ml, though this varies by bottle and atomizer. Using that estimate, a 100 ml bottle at 2.5 sprays per wear gives roughly 400 wears in theory. Real-world factors like testing losses, spills, and evaporation typically bring that down to somewhere between 80 and 150 wears for most people. That's a wide range, which is exactly why logging your actual wears rather than guessing matters so much.

## How does concentration change the math?

*A question we hear often:* does buying a parfum instead of an EDT actually cost more, or does the math work out?

Bottle size, sprays per wear, and concentration all change the calculation. A parfum needs fewer sprays than an EDT, so the value gap is often smaller than the price tag suggests. In practice:

- EDT: typically 2-4 sprays per wear, lighter oil concentration
- EDP: typically 2-3 sprays per wear, stronger projection
- Parfum/Extrait: typically 1-2 sprays per wear, highest oil load

That means a 50 ml parfum at $250, used at 1.5 sprays per wear, gives you roughly 333 wears at a cost per wear of about $0.75. A 100 ml EDT at $95, used at 3 sprays per wear, gives approximately 333 wears too, at about $0.29 per wear. The parfum costs 2.6x more to buy but only 2.6x more per wear because the math balances. Factor in that parfum often projects longer and you're less likely to reapply mid-day, and the real-world gap can narrow even further.

## What's the difference between cost per wear and cost per ml - do I need both?

*This one comes up a lot:* collectors sometimes conflate the two, but they answer different questions.

Cost per ml tells you how efficiently a bottle is priced for the liquid inside. It's useful when comparing two sizes of the same fragrance, or spotting how much of a luxury markup is pure packaging. It strips out bottle size entirely and shows you what you're actually paying for the liquid.

Cost per wear tells you what each occasion of wearing actually costs you, and it depends on your personal spray habits, which cost-per-ml completely ignores.

Use cost per ml when you're deciding which size to buy. Use cost per wear when you're deciding whether to buy at all, or when comparing two different fragrances for your wardrobe. A low per-ml rate on a scent you tire of isn't a saving, which is why cost per wear is the more honest metric of the two.

## How does wear frequency affect whether an expensive bottle is "worth it"?

*Readers frequently ask:* I keep buying fragrance but feel guilty about the cost. How do I actually know if a bottle is earning its keep?

Wear frequency is the decisive variable. Consider two collectors who each own a $250 niche EDP (100 ml):

- Collector A reaches for it four times a week. At 2.5 sprays per wear and 850 realistic wears in the bottle, they finish it in roughly 4 years and pay about $0.29 per wear.
- Collector B saves it for special occasions, say twice a month. Same 850 wears, but at that pace they'd theoretically take 35 years to finish the bottle. The fragrance will likely degrade long before then. Their real wears might number 100-150 before the scent turns, putting their effective cost per wear closer to $1.67-$2.50.

The math illustrates something every experienced collector knows intuitively: rotation anxiety, the habit of saving your best bottles "for the right moment," is actually the most expensive habit in fragrance collecting. Wearing your wardrobe regularly isn't indulgence, it's sound economics. This is precisely why building a [perfume rotation system](/how-to-rotate-your-perfume-collection) matters for collectors with more than a handful of bottles. If a bottle sits unworn for months, its cost per wear is quietly climbing every day.

## How do I account for testing and wasted sprays in the calculation?

Most collectors miss this step. Between the initial sillage test in-store, the spray onto your wrist to check the dry-down, and the occasional over-spray, real-world yield from a bottle is meaningfully lower than the theoretical maximum.

A practical adjustment: reduce your gross spray count by 10-20% before dividing by sprays per wear. For a bottle you sampled heavily before buying, use 15-20%. For a blind buy you opened and immediately started wearing, 10% is realistic. If you also use the bottle to fill a travel decant, account for the ml transferred.

The adjusted formula looks like this:

> Realistic wears = (Total sprays x 0.85) / Sprays per wear

This gives a conservative estimate that holds up against real-world usage better than the pure theoretical figure. ## Does cost per wear change how I should think about buying sample sets or decants?

*A question we hear often:* is it ever smarter to buy a decant rather than a full bottle, purely from a cost-per-wear perspective?

It depends on two things: how confident you are you'll love the fragrance long-term, and how often you'll actually reach for it. A 5 ml decant at $20 gives you roughly 50 sprays, or about 20 wears at 2.5 sprays each, at a cost per wear of $1.00. A full 100 ml bottle of the same scent at $180 gives you ~340 realistic wears at $0.53 per wear. Half the cost per occasion.

But only if you actually wear all 340 of those occasions. If you discover after 30 wears that you've lost interest, the 5 ml decant was the smarter call even though its per-wear cost was higher. Your exit cost was capped at $20 rather than the $174 sunk into an unfinished bottle.

The practical rule: use samples and decants (covered in more depth in our [perfume samples vs full bottles guide](/perfume-samples-vs-full-bottles)) to build confidence in a fragrance before calculating cost per wear on a full purchase. Only commit to the bottle when you're confident your projected wear frequency is sustainable.

## 5 quick FAQs on perfume cost per wear

What is a good cost per wear for perfume?
Most collectors consider under $1.00 per wear excellent value, $1.00-$2.50 reasonable for niche or luxury bottles, and anything above $3.00 a sign the bottle isn't being worn enough to justify its place in the wardrobe.

How many sprays are in a 100ml perfume bottle?
Using the standard estimate of 10 sprays per ml, a 100 ml bottle contains roughly 1,000 sprays. Real-world factors like atomizer design, testing losses, and evaporation typically reduce effective wears, so plan on 800-850 usable sprays as your working figure.

Does a parfum concentration cost less per wear than an EDT?
Usually yes. Because parfum carries a higher oil concentration, 1-2 sprays are typically sufficient per wear versus 2-4 for an EDT. That lower spray-per-use count means the gap between an expensive parfum and a cheaper EDT is often narrower in cost-per-wear terms than the retail price implies.

How does wear frequency affect perfume value?
It's the single biggest variable. A $300 bottle worn three times a week finishes in roughly a year and costs around $1.75 per wear. The same bottle worn once a month takes years to finish, risks degradation before you get there, and pushes real cost per wear significantly higher.
